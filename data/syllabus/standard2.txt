12.9.1.2 Identify and define the sample space as the set of all possible outcomes of a chance experiment
12.9.1.3 Determine the number of outcomes for an experiment
12.9.1.4 Express the probability of an event that has a finite number of equally likely outcomes, as $P(\text{event})=\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$
12.9.1.5 Apply $P(\text{an event does not occur})=1-P(\text{the event does occur})$ to calculate the probability of the complement of an event
12.9.1.6 Construct and use arrays, tree diagrams and probability trees to determine the outcomes and probabilities for multistage events
12.9.1.7 Determine the probabilities of outcomes for multistage events using $P(\text{A and B})=P(A)\times P(B)$
12.9.1.8 Use relative frequency as an estimate of probability
12.9.1.9 Recognise that increasing the number of trials produces relative frequencies that become closer in value to the theoretical probability
12.9.1.10 Calculate the expected frequency of an event occurring using $np$ where $n$ is the number of times an experiment is repeated, and $p$ is the probability that the event occurs on each of those times
12.9.1.11 Construct and interpret Venn diagrams and two-way tables from given information, limited to combinations of two attributes, and use them to solve problems in a variety of contexts
12.9.1.12 Examine the use of statistics and the associated probabilities in shaping decisions made by the media, governments and companies
## 12.10 The normal distribution
### 12.10.1 Normally distributed datasets
12.10.1.1 Recognise that a dataset that is normally distributed can be represented by a bell-shaped curve
12.10.1.2 Explain that the mean, median and mode are approximately equal for data arising from a random variable that is normally distributed
### 12.10.2 Calculating z-scores
12.10.2.1 Describe the $z$-score as the number of standard deviations that a value is above or below the mean
12.10.2.2 Recognise that the set of $z$-scores for data arising from a random variable that is normally distributed has a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1
12.10.2.3 Calculate the $z$-score corresponding to a specific value in a dataset by applying the formula $z=\frac{x-\mu }{\sigma }$, where $x$ is a specific value, $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation
12.10.2.4 Use $z$-scores to compare scores from different datasets and justify conclusions in the context of the problem
12.10.2.5 Model and apply the empirical rule, where approximately 68% of data will have $z$-scores between −1 and 1, approximately 95% of data will have $z$-scores between −2 and 2, and approximately 99.7% of data will have $z$-scores between −3 and 3
### 12.10.3 Probability using z-scores
12.10.3.1 Calculate probabilities using $z$-scores and the empirical rule
12.10.3.2 Represent probabilities by shading areas under the normal distribution curve
12.10.3.3 Use $z$-scores to identify probabilities of events less or more extreme than a given event and solve problems
12.10.3.4 Use $z$-scores to make judgements related to outcomes of a given event or sets of data
